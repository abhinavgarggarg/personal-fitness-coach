Prepared 9 Oct 2026 for the Personal Fitness Coach (offline training engine). Research only: nothing in the repository was changed.

## 1. Key findings by question

### Q1. Exercise without a prescribed diet: expected weight, fat and waist loss by dose

**Aerobic dose-response [Jayedi2024] (checked):**

- **Scope:** 116 RCTs, 6,880 adults with overweight or obesity, about 61% women. Mean age was 46 years (SD 13), and trial mean ages ranged from 19 to 74. All trials lasted at least 8 weeks.
- **Effect per extra 30 min/week of aerobic exercise:**

| Outcome | Change per 30 min/week (95% CI) | Trials | GRADE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Body weight | −0.52 kg (−0.61 to −0.44) | 109 | moderate |
| Waist | −0.56 cm (−0.67 to −0.45) | 62 | high |
| Body fat | −0.37 percentage points (−0.43 to −0.31) | 65 | moderate |
| Visceral fat area | −1.60 cm² (−2.12 to −1.07) | 26 | high |
| Subcutaneous fat area | −1.37 cm² (−1.82 to −0.92) | 27 | moderate |

- **Shape:** linear or monotonic, up to 300 min/week.
- **Predicted effect at a given weekly dose:**

| Weekly aerobic dose | Body weight (95% CI) | Waist | Body fat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 150 min/week | −2.79 kg (−3.29 to −2.29) | not given | −2.08 percentage points |
| 300 min/week | −4.19 kg (−5.98 to −2.41) | −4.21 cm (moderate intensity); −5.34 cm (moderate-to-vigorous) | not given |

- **Clinical importance:**
  - The paper's thresholds for a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) are 2 cm waist, 2 percentage points body fat and 2 kg fat mass.
  - Fat mass reached its MCID at about 100 min/week. Body fat % reached it at about 150 min/week.
  - The authors conclude that more than 150 min/week at moderate or greater intensity is probably needed for clinically important reductions.
- **Moderators:** no credible difference by intensity or sex was reported, and age was not analysed. Calorie restriction as a co-intervention was a post hoc subgroup with no credible difference reported, and dietary data were sparse.
- **Harms:** aerobic exercise added about 2 mild-to-moderate adverse events per 100 participants (27 vs 46 per 1,000; RR 1.74), mostly musculoskeletal: knee, ankle and back.
- **Caveat:** the effects accumulated over each trial's full length, not per week. The report does not give a median trial duration.

**Overview of reviews [Bellicha2021] (checked; full text read):**

- **Scope:** 12 systematic reviews or meta-analyses covering 149 studies. Most compared exercise with no exercise; only 3 reviews involved a weight-loss diet.
- **Exercise vs control:**
  - Weight: −1.5 to −3.5 kg.
  - Fat mass: −1.3 to −2.6 kg.
  - Visceral fat: SMD −0.33 to −0.56.
- **Exercise volumes:** trials ranged from 60 to 440 min/week, and most used 150–200 min/week.
- **Resistance training (RT):** not effective for weight loss vs control. RT was the only modality without a significant visceral-fat reduction.
- **Weight maintenance:** no evidence from RCTs that exercise prevents regain.
  - Retrospective analyses suggest at least 250 min/week may be needed.
  - The METPOWeR trial (150 vs 225 vs 300 min/week) found no significant difference between doses; regain was 1.1–2.8 kg.

**EASO recommendations [Oppert2021] (checked; full text read):**

- **Weight loss:** 150–200 min/week of aerobic exercise at moderate intensity or more (Grade A). Moderate means about 5–6 on a 0–10 effort scale, or 50–70% of maximum heart rate.
- **Expected result:** an average weight loss of no more than about 2–3 kg.
- **Weight maintenance:** 200–300 min/week of moderate exercise, graded E (expert opinion).
- **Lean mass during weight loss:** moderate-to-high intensity RT (above 60% of 1RM) is advised (Grade A).

**ACSM 2009 position stand [Donnelly2009] (abstract checked through a repository copy):**

- 150–250 min/week prevents weight gain but gives only modest weight loss.
- More than 250 min/week is associated with clinically significant weight loss. It is also linked to better maintenance, but that evidence comes from cross-sectional and prospective data, not RCTs.
- RT does not enhance weight loss, but it may increase fat-free mass and fat loss.
- A secondary summary (Kravitz/IDEA, not the original) gives further bands from the body text:
  - under 150 min/week: minimal loss
  - over 150 min/week: about 2–3 kg
  - 225–420 min/week: 5–7.5 kg

  I could not open the full text to confirm these bands, so treat them as **located**.

**Earlier and newer ACSM statements:**

- The 2001 predecessor [Jakicic2001] (checked) recommended 200–300 min/week for long-term weight-loss maintenance.
- The newest ACSM statement is the 2024 consensus statement [Jakicic2024] (checked, abstract only). I found no position stand that replaces Donnelly 2009. The 2024 statement says:
  - Benefits for weight loss and preventing weight gain appear when activity is progressed to at least 150 min/week of moderate intensity, in a dose-response manner.
  - HIIT does not appear superior to moderate-to-vigorous activity for weight regulation.
  - Light-intensity activity may be an alternative if total energy expenditure is enough.
  - No single mode is superior for weight; multimodal activity is recommended.

**Thresholds the app can use (my synthesis):**

| Weekly aerobic dose | What to expect without a diet |
|---|---|
| <150 min | Small change: about 0.5 kg per 30 min/week, waist about 0.6 cm per 30 min/week |
| ≈150 min | About −2.8 kg and body fat about −2 percentage points over a programme (MCID for body fat % and fat mass reached) |
| 200–300 min | About −4 kg and waist −4 to −5 cm at 300 min/week |
| ≥250 min | Linked to better maintenance in observational data only. RCTs do not show that exercise prevents regain. |

### Q2. HIIT/SIT vs moderate continuous training (MICT) for fat loss

- **[Viana2019] (checked):** 36 studies in the meta-analysis.
  - Within-group change in body fat: interval training −1.50 percentage points; MICT −1.44.
  - Between groups, body fat % did not differ: −0.23 (95% CI −1.43 to 0.97; p=0.705).
  - Absolute fat mass favoured interval training: −2.28 kg (−4.00 to −0.56). In the sprint interval (SIT) subgroup it was −3.22 kg.
  - The authors describe this as "28.5% greater" fat-mass reduction.
  - *Caution:* the between-group kg difference is larger than either within-group change (−1.58 and −1.13 kg). The analyses used different study subsets, so treat the kg advantage as fragile.
- **[Wewege2017] (located; findings via Medical Xpress press summary):** 13 trials, about 3 sessions/week for about 10 weeks.
  - HIIT and MICT gave similar body-composition changes.
  - HIIT took about 40% less training time.
  - Running-based training reduced fat; cycling did not.
- **[SancaValeriano2023] (checked):** 30 studies, interventions of 4–16 weeks. There was no HIIT vs MICT difference for:

| Outcome | p |
|---|---|
| Body fat % | 0.07 |
| Fat mass | 0.39 |
| Waist | 0.87 |
| Weight | 0.58 |
| BMI | 0.53 |

  Only insulin sensitivity favoured HIIT (p=0.02).
- **[Bellicha2021] (checked):** at matched energy expenditure, weight and fat loss were similar. Mean differences were at most 700 g either way.
- **[Jakicic2024] (checked):** HIIT is not superior for body-weight regulation.
- **Conclusion (High–Moderate Evidence):** HIIT is time-efficient but not superior for fat loss. The app should treat HIIT as one way to accumulate weekly aerobic minutes, never as a fat-loss accelerator.

### Q3. Role of resistance training

- **[Lopez2022] (checked):** 114 RCTs, 4,184 people with overweight or obesity.
  - **RT alone vs no training:**
    - Body fat −1.6 percentage points.
    - Fat mass −1.0 kg.
    - Lean mass +0.8 kg (95% CI 0.6 to 1.0).
  - **RT plus aerobic vs no training:** body fat −2.3 percentage points; fat mass −1.4 kg.
  - **RT plus caloric restriction vs no intervention:**
    - Body fat −3.8 percentage points.
    - Fat mass −5.3 kg.
    - Lean mass was maintained: about −0.3 kg, not significant.
  - Results were described as consistent across age and sex, but the abstract gives no age-specific numbers.
- **[Bellicha2021] (checked):** RT is the best modality for preserving lean mass during diet-induced weight loss. Aerobic training is better than RT for weight, fat and visceral-fat loss.
- **[Sardeli2018] (checked):** 6 RCTs in older adults with obesity. Bellicha reports their mean age as over 57.
  - RT was done 3×/week for 12–24 weeks during caloric restriction.
  - RT prevented 93.5% of the diet-induced lean-mass loss: +0.82 kg (95% CI 0.36 to 1.27) vs restriction alone.
  - Fat and weight loss were similar with or without RT.
- **[Villareal2017] (checked):** 160 dieting older adults with obesity (141 completed). All exercise groups lost about 9% of body weight. The inclusion age of ≥65 is from my memory (unverified).

| Group | Lean mass | Total hip BMD | Physical Performance Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aerobic only | −5% | −3% | +14% |
| Resistance only | −2% | −0.5% | +14% |
| Combined | −3% | −1% | +21% |

- **[Willis2012] (checked, abstract; protocol details from Duke press release):** 119 completers over 8 months.
  - **Protocols:**
    - Aerobic: about 12 miles/week, about 133 min/week.
    - RT: 3 days/week, 3 sets of 8–12 reps, about 180 min/week.
    - Combined: both, at double the time commitment.
  - **Results:**
    - Aerobic and combined training reduced body and fat mass more than RT; combined was no better than aerobic.
    - RT and combined training increased lean mass more than aerobic training.
    - Combined training gave the largest waist decrease (press release).
- **[Peterson2011] (located; findings via press summary):** adults aged ≥50, 49 studies, about 20 weeks of RT. Lean mass rose by about 1.1 kg, and higher volume (sets) was associated with larger gains.
- **ACSM 2026 resistance-training position stand [ACSM2026]** (ACSM press page and infographic checked this session; citation details from the project registry):
  - Train all major muscle groups at least 2 days/week.
  - Hypertrophy: about 10 sets per muscle group per week.
  - Strength: 2–3 sets per exercise at about 80% of 1RM.
  - Power: 30–70% of 1RM, lifted as fast as possible.
  - Training to failure is not needed. Equipment type and complex periodisation did not consistently matter.
- **Implication:** combine aerobic work (the main driver of fat loss) with RT (the main driver of lean mass). The RT dose should not drop with age. Lean-mass protection matters most whenever weight is falling, and users may restrict food on their own even though the app gives no diet.

### Q4. Age and muscle

**[Mitchell2012] quantitative review (checked; full text read):**

- **Muscle mass, cross-sectional:**
  - Median loss about 0.47%/year in men and 0.37%/year in women (18–45 vs >65 years).
  - Per decade vs peak: about 4.7% in men and 3.7% in women.
  - Total loss between 18 and 80 years ranges from 8% to 49% across studies.
- **Muscle mass, longitudinal (around age 75):** men 0.80–0.98%/year; women 0.64–0.70%/year.
- **Strength:**
  - Around age 75: men lose 3–4%/year and women 2.5–3%/year.
  - Strength falls **2–5 times faster than mass**.
  - In one 5-year study: men lost 16% of knee-extensor strength vs 5% of thigh mass; women lost 13% vs 3%.
- **Age of onset** varies by study: 27 years (Silva 2009), 45 years [Janssen2000] or 60 years (Kyle 2001).
  - Mass is roughly stable for much of adult life.
  - After 70, average loss is about 0.5–1%/year.
  - The widely quoted "1–2%/year after 50" is described as misattributed.
- Loss of strength is a more consistent risk for disability and death than loss of mass.

**Verdict on the popular "3–8% per decade after 30" claim:**

- It is **not supported as stated** by the best quantitative review.
- Pooled cross-sectional data give about 3.7–4.7% per decade relative to peak, without a clear start at 30.
- Onset estimates range from the late 20s to 60.
- Decline accelerates after about 60–70, and strength and power fall faster than mass.
- The claim is usually attributed to [Volpi2004] (unverified; I could not open it).
- **App wording:** "muscle strength and mass decline gradually through adulthood, faster after about 60–70; strength falls faster than mass."

**WHO 2020 [Bull2020]** (located; wording verified via a verbatim reproduction, as the BJSM and WHO pages were blocked):

- **Adults 18–64 and older adults 65+ (same targets):**
  - 150–300 min/week moderate aerobic activity, or 75–150 min/week vigorous, or an equivalent mix. Going above these gives additional benefit.
  - Muscle strengthening of all major muscle groups at moderate or greater intensity, ≥2 days/week.
  - Limit sedentary time.
- **Older adults 65+ additionally:** varied **multicomponent activity emphasising functional balance and strength training at moderate or greater intensity on ≥3 days/week**, to improve function and prevent falls.

**NSCA [Fragala2019]** (abstract checked; dosing via secondary summary, located):

- Resistance training is "a powerful intervention" against age-related losses of muscle mass, strength and function.
- Dosing as summarised secondarily: 2–3 sessions/week, 2–3 sets of 1–2 exercises per major muscle group, 70–85% of 1RM.

**ICFSR 2021 [Izquierdo2021]** (abstract only, no numbers):

- Exercise for older adults should be individualised and dosed like a medical treatment.
- It shows a dose-response effect.

### Q5. HIIT safety, tolerability and frequency in middle-aged and older adults and with overweight

- **[Rognmo2012] (located; numbers via ACC Journal Scan):**
  - 4,846 people with coronary heart disease in cardiac rehab, mean age 57.8.
  - 1 event per 23,182 h of high-intensity intervals (2 non-fatal cardiac arrests) vs 1 per 129,456 h of moderate exercise (1 fatal arrest). There were no heart attacks.
  - Absolute risk was low in a supervised setting.
- **[Wewege2018] (checked):** 23 studies, 1,117 patients with coronary disease or heart failure, 17,083 HIIT sessions.
  - 1 major cardiovascular event, about 1 per 17,083 sessions.
  - Non-cardiovascular adverse events were few (3 with HIIT vs 2 with MICT), mostly musculoskeletal.
- **[Stensvold2020] Generation 100 (checked):**
  - 1,567 adults aged 70–77 did HIIT **2×/week at about 90% of peak heart rate for 5 years**.
  - Mortality vs controls: HR 0.63 (0.33–1.20), not significant.
  - This shows HIIT twice weekly is feasible long-term at this age. The abstract gives no safety data.
- **[Men2025] (checked):** 87 RCTs, 4,213 older adults; mean ages about 63–67.
  - Adverse events: 46 (about 1.09%), none attributed to HIIT by the authors.
  - Withdrawals: about 4.65%.
  - Supervision was not described in most studies.
  - Body fat: −1.63 percentage points vs control. BMI and waist changes were not significant.
- **Obesity-specific safety:**
  - [Bellicha2021] notes a lack of HIIT safety data in adults with obesity.
  - [Oppert2021] (EASO) advises HIIT only **after a thorough cardiovascular risk assessment and under supervision** (Grade B).
- **Aerobic exercise in general [Jayedi2024]:** about 2 extra mild-to-moderate musculoskeletal adverse events per 100 people.
- **Screening [Riebe2015] (checked, abstract):** ACSM pre-exercise screening rests on three things: current activity level, known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease or its signs and symptoms, and the intended exercise intensity.
- **Joint load and body mass [Messier2005] (located; secondary):**
  - In older adults with overweight or obesity and knee osteoarthritis, each pound lost removed about 4 pounds of knee load.
  - This is indirect support for low-impact formats while body mass is high.
- **Heart-rate formula [Tanaka2001] (checked):**
  - HRmax ≈ 208 − 0.7 × age.
  - "220 − age" underestimates HRmax in older adults by about 10 beats/min at age 70.
  - This matters only if typed-in heart rate is used; the app's talk test and CR10 effort scale avoid the problem.
- **No source I found sets HIIT frequency by age or body mass.** The app's caps (≤3 below 50, ≤2 from 50) are expert or product rules. Generation 100 used 2/week at 70–77.
- **Reducing high-impact work with age or body mass:** I found no direct guideline. The reasonable basis is:
  - indirect evidence: musculoskeletal adverse events [Jayedi2024] and knee loading [Messier2005]
  - osteoporosis guidance: impact limited to brisk walking for people with vertebral fractures [BrookeWavell2022]
  - EASO's caution on HIIT [Oppert2021]

  Labelled **Expert Practice**.

### Q6. Daily steps

**Mortality dose-response [Paluch2022] (checked):**

- 15 cohorts, 47,471 adults, 3,013 deaths, median follow-up 7.1 years.

| Step quartile (median steps/day) | All-cause mortality HR vs Q1 (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| Q1: 3,553 | 1 (reference) |
| Q2: 5,801 | 0.60 (0.51–0.71) |
| Q3: 7,842 | 0.55 (0.49–0.62) |
| Q4: 10,901 | 0.47 (0.39–0.57) |

- **Benefit levels off at:**
  - **6,000–8,000 steps/day for adults ≥60**
  - **8,000–10,000 steps/day for adults <60**
- **Cadence:** after adjusting for total steps, time at ≥100 steps/min was not significant (HR 0.86, 0.58–1.28). The highest 30-minute cadence was significant (HR 0.67). Total steps matter most.

**Range of outcomes [Ding2025]** (located; numbers via ACC Journal Scan and lifespan.io, Lancet page blocked):

- 57 studies.
- **7,000 vs 2,000 steps/day:**

| Outcome | Risk reduction |
|---|---|
| All-cause mortality | 47% lower |
| Cardiovascular incidence | 25% lower |
| Cardiovascular mortality | 47% lower |
| Cancer mortality | 37% lower |
| Dementia | 38% lower |
| Type 2 diabetes | 14% lower |
| Depressive symptoms | 22% lower |
| Falls | 28% lower |

- Going from 2,000 to 4,000 steps/day: about 36% lower mortality.
- Many outcomes levelled off around 5,000–7,000 steps/day.
- The cardiovascular-incidence inflection point was 7,802 steps/day overall vs about 5,400 in older adults.

**US cohort [SaintMaurice2020]** (located; rates via CMA POEM summary):

- 4,840 NHANES adults.
- All-cause deaths per 1,000 person-years by steps/day:

| Steps/day | Deaths per 1,000 person-years |
|---|---|
| <4,000 | 76.7 |
| 4,000–7,999 | 21.4 |
| 8,000–11,999 | 6.9 |
| ≥12,000 | 4.8 |

- Step intensity was not associated with mortality after adjusting for total steps.
- The hazard ratios (about 0.49 for 8,000 vs 4,000 steps) are from my memory, so **unverified**.

**Steps and weight:**

- [Richardson2008] (checked): 9 studies, 307 people, median 16 weeks.
  - Pedometer walking gave −1.27 kg (−1.85 to −0.70), about **0.05 kg/week**.
  - Longer programmes produced more loss.
- [Bravata2007] (checked, abstract):
  - Pedometer programmes added +2,491 steps/day vs control (+26.9% over baseline) over a mean of 18 weeks.
  - BMI fell 0.38; systolic blood pressure fell 3.8 mmHg.
  - **Having a step goal was an important predictor** of increased activity.

**Translating guidelines into steps:**

- [TudorLocke2011a] (checked):
  - 30 min of moderate-to-vigorous activity is about 3,000 steps at ≥100 steps/min.
  - 7,000–8,000 steps/day roughly equals the minimum guideline amount.
  - Pedometer interventions raise steps by about 2,000–2,500/day.
- [TudorLocke2011b] (checked):
  - Healthy older adults typically take 2,000–9,000 steps/day.
  - 7,000–10,000 steps/day roughly equals the guideline for them.
  - Interventions raised older adults' steps by only about **775/day**.
  - 100 steps/min may be unattainable for some frail people.

**How fast to raise a step target:**

- No guideline gives a weekly progression rate.
- In interventions, the average total gain is about 2,000–2,500 steps/day over about 4 months; that is roughly +130–150 per week on average.
- [Adams2013] (checked; small RCT, n=20, mean age 37): **adaptive, individual percentile-based goals** beat a fixed 10,000 target (+2,728 vs +1,598 steps/day; d=0.74).
- So any weekly increment (+250 to +1,000 steps/day) is **Expert Practice**. Adaptive goals have Limited Evidence support.

### Q7. Measuring progress without nutrition features

**Weighing [Madigan2015] (checked):**

| Approach | Weight difference (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| Self-weighing alone (1 trial) | −0.5 kg (−1.3 to 0.3), not significant |
| Self-weighing added to a programme (4 trials) | −1.7 kg (−2.6 to −0.8) |
| Programmes including self-weighing vs minimal control (15 trials) | −3.4 kg (−4.2 to −2.6) |

- **There was no significant difference between daily and weekly weighing.**
- Accountability improved results.

**Weekly rhythm [Orsama2014]** (located; the findings text was truncated):

- Weight is highest on Sunday and Monday and falls through the week.
- This supports **7-day averaging**, not reacting to single readings.
- Note: Brian Wansink is a co-author, and several of his other papers were retracted. I have not checked whether this one is affected, so the rationale for averaging should rest mainly on general measurement noise.

**Waist protocol — WHO STEPS** (located; protocol via the MRC Epidemiology measurement toolkit):

- **Site:** midpoint between the lowest palpable rib and the top of the iliac crest.
- **Posture:** standing, feet slightly apart, arms relaxed, not holding the stomach in.
- **Timing:** end of a normal breath out, fasted.
- **Tape:** non-stretch, snug, horizontal.
- **Reading:** the toolkit's general procedure is three readings averaged, to 0.1 cm.
- The WHO 2008 expert-consultation report [WHO2011waist] (located) is the parent document; its protocol was not visible on the page I opened.

**Indian consensus [Misra2025] (checked):**

- **Waist ≥90 cm in men and ≥80 cm in women** marks abdominal obesity. A waist-to-height ratio above 0.5 is also used, and BMI above 23 defines generalised obesity in Asian Indians.
- **Its measuring site differs from WHO STEPS:** just above the iliac crest, end of normal expiration, fasting, standing.
- **Physical activity:** at least 60 min/day, resistance exercise on ≥3 days/week (ideally daily), and reference to WHO's 150–300 min/week.
- **Safe rate of loss:** **0.5–1 kg/week**, cited in the context of a roughly 500 kcal/day deficit, i.e. with a diet.

**IDF South Asian cut-offs [IDF2006]** (located; via Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health page):

| Group | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| South Asians and Asian Indians | ≥90 cm | ≥80 cm |
| Europids, for comparison | ≥94 cm | ≥80 cm |

**Realistic rate for this app (no diet):**

- Exercise-only programmes give about 2–3 kg on average [Oppert2021], or about 2.8 kg at 150 min/week [Jayedi2024].
- If that accrues over about 3–6 months, it is roughly 0.1–0.25 kg/week. This is my arithmetic, because trial durations are not reported.
- Pedometer walking alone gives about 0.05 kg/week [Richardson2008].
- **Waist is the better primary outcome:** RT adds lean mass (+0.8 kg [Lopez2022]), so the scale can stall while fat falls.
- Sustained loss above 1 kg/week is outside what exercise alone explains and above the consensus "safe" ceiling, so it should be flagged (Expert Practice).

### Q8. Women around menopause (about 40–55)

- **[Greendale2019] SWAN (checked):** 1,246 women; mean age at final menstrual period (FMP) 52.2.

| Per year | Premenopause | During the menopause transition |
|---|---|---|
| Fat mass | +0.25 kg | +0.45 kg |
| Lean mass | +0.06 kg | −0.06 kg |

  - These changes begin about 2 years before the FMP and stabilise after the transition.
  - Weight gain did **not** accelerate (about 0.25 kg/year). Body composition worsens even when weight looks stable.
  - Waist and visceral fat were not measured.
- **Menopause society guidance:**
  - IMS 2025 factsheet [IMS2025] (checked): at least 150 min/week moderate aerobic activity plus resistance training on ≥2 days/week. Combined aerobic and resistance training is described as best for symptoms, strength and fat reduction.
  - EMAS Menopause Essentials 2024 and 2026 [EMAS2024_2026] (checked): restate WHO adult guidance and give no menopause-specific dosing.
- **Bone (osteoporosis consensus) [BrookeWavell2022]** (located; two secondary summaries):
  - Muscle strengthening 2–3 days/week, building to up to 3 sets of 8–12 reps at a challenging load.
  - Moderate impact (about 50 impacts per session) on most days.
  - Balance training twice weekly.
  - If someone has vertebral fractures, impact is capped at brisk walking and loaded end-range spinal flexion is avoided.
- **[Watson2018] LIFTMOR (checked):** 101 postmenopausal women with low bone mass, aged 65 ± 5.
  - Supervised high-intensity resistance and impact training: 2×/week, 30 min, 5×5 at >85% of 1RM, for 8 months.

| Measure | Training group | Controls | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lumbar spine BMD | +2.9% | −1.2% | <0.001 |
| Femoral neck BMD | +0.3% | −1.9% | 0.004 |

  - One minor adverse event occurred (back spasm), under close supervision.
- **[Whitman2025] (checked, abstract):** 6 RCTs in women aged 45–60.
  - In perimenopause, exercise did not improve bone density or lean mass.
  - In early postmenopause, strength training improved bone density.
  - All studies were low quality, so the evidence is inconclusive.
- **[GonzalezGalvez2024] (checked):** 12 RCTs of RT in postmenopausal women.
  - Strength and VO2max improved.
  - There were no pooled effects on body mass, BMI or bone density.
  - The pooled effect sizes are implausibly large (SMD 4.7–7.4), so use with caution.
- **Implication:** no menopause-specific body-composition prescription exists. The defensible rule is:
  - Keep ≥2 strength days (aim for 3) with progressive loading through 40–55.
  - Add bone-loading impact only if there is no osteoporosis or vertebral-fracture flag.
  - Frame "stable weight with a falling waist and rising strength" as success.

## 2. Proposed age-banded weekly plan for "Lose fat, keep muscle" (MY PROPOSAL)

**Built to fit the app's existing rules:**

- **Zones (AER-001):**
  - Z1 easy counts as moderate.
  - Z2 tempo and Z3 intervals count as vigorous.
- **WHO accounting (PH-001):** equivalent minutes = Z1 + 2 × (Z2 + Z3 work).
- **HIIT:**
  - Ceilings per HIIT-001 and AGE-001: ≤3/week below 50 (the 3rd only under HIIT-001's conditions); ≤2/week from 50.
  - Spacing and prerequisites per HIIT-003 and HIIT-004.
- **Aerobic progression:** at most +15% per week (AER-003).
- **Strength:** at least 2 days/week.
- **Modalities (MOD-001):** treadmill running and stationary bikes are excluded by default. "Low-impact" therefore means rower, SkiErg, elliptical and sled push, plus outdoor or hill walking.
- **Time:** with the default 3 × 60 min gym week, most Z1 minutes will have to come from walking. Steps and steady cardio are deliberately linked (see the step rules below).

**Design logic:**

- **Aerobic minutes are the fat-loss driver**, with a linear dose-response to 300 min/week [Jayedi2024]. The moderate (Z1) volume therefore stays the same across ages, at the WHO 150–300 range.
- **What changes with age is the mix:**
  - Strength frequency rises to 3/week and stays there.
  - Power is added from 50.
  - Vigorous and HIIT share falls, and impact falls.
  - Balance is added from 50 and is required at 3 days/week from 65.

**Goal-wide rules (all ages; my proposal):**

- **G-1 Expectation messaging** (**High** for the numbers; wording is a product rule):
  - Without diet changes, expect roughly 2–3 kg of weight loss and a 2–5 cm waist reduction over a few months at 150–300 min/week.
  - Never promise faster loss.
  - Sources: [Jayedi2024, Oppert2021, Bellicha2021].
- **G-2 Primary outcome = waist trend + strength trend.** Weight is secondary (**Moderate**: RT adds lean mass [Lopez2022]; menopause fat-lean shift [Greendale2019]).
- **G-3 Weighing:**
  - Optional, daily or at least weekly, under the same conditions (morning, after the toilet, before eating or drinking).
  - The app shows a 7-day mean and judges only 4-week trends.
  - Confidence: daily vs weekly is equivalent (**Moderate** [Madigan2015]); averaging and conditions are **Expert Practice**.
- **G-4 Waist measurement:**
  - Every 2–4 weeks, using the WHO STEPS site, 3 readings averaged to 0.1 cm.
  - Use the same site every time, and say which site in the UI.
  - Show the South Asian reference lines (≥90 cm men, ≥80 cm women) as information only, not as a diagnosis.
  - Confidence: protocol is consensus/**Expert Practice**; cut-offs are **Moderate** (consensus) [Misra2025, IDF2006].
- **G-5 Rate flag:**
  - If the 7-day-mean weight falls more than 1 kg/week for 3 consecutive weeks, show a neutral check-in ("faster than exercise alone usually explains; if you are not trying to diet or feel unwell, consider checking with a doctor").
  - No food advice, as nutrition is out of scope.
  - Confidence: **Expert Practice**; ceiling from [Misra2025].
- **G-6 HIIT gate for BMI ≥30 or age ≥60:**
  - Screening must be clear (SAF-001 / [Riebe2015] logic).
  - Low-impact modalities only.
  - First block uses short 1:2 or long intervals at CR10 7–8 (HIIT-003).
  - Confidence: **Expert Practice**, informed by [Oppert2021] Grade B and [Bellicha2021] on missing safety data.

**Step rules (deterministic sketch; my proposal, Expert Practice unless stated):**

- **S-1 Baseline:**
  - The median of the first 7 valid days. A day is valid if the phone recorded ≥500 steps; this threshold is a product rule.
  - First target = baseline + one weekly increment (S-2), never above the band's end-target.
- **S-2 Weekly increment:**
  - increment = clamp(round_to_250(p × mean7), 250, cap_band)
    - p = 0.15 below 50, 0.10 from 50
    - cap_band = 1,000 below 50; 750 for 50–59; 500 for 60+
    - multiply by 0.8 for 65+ (AGE-001)
  - Advance only if the target was met on ≥5 of 7 days.
  - Hold if it was met on 3–4 days.
  - Lower the target by one increment after 2 consecutive weeks with ≤2 days met.
  - No increase during a deload week or while a lower-limb pain flag is active.
- **S-3 Linking steps to aerobic minutes (avoids double counting in PH-001):**
  - Only *brisk-walk bouts* count as Z1 minutes: ≥10 consecutive minutes at ≥100 steps/min.
  - The cadence threshold is **Moderate** [TudorLocke2011a]; the bout rule is **Expert Practice**.
  - All other steps count only toward the step target.
  - Whether the phone sensor can resolve per-minute cadence offline is an open engineering question.
- **S-4 Weekly aerobic-volume guard:**
  - Brisk-walk minutes count toward the AER-003 ≤15%/week growth limit.
  - Ordinary steps do not.

**Which numbers come straight from evidence vs extrapolation:**

| Number in the plan | Origin |
|---|---|
| 150–300 equivalent min/week; ≥2 strength days; ≥3 balance+strength days at 65+ | Guideline [Bull2020] |
| Linear fat/waist benefit to 300 min/week; ≈150 min for clinically important change | Meta-analysis [Jayedi2024] |
| HIIT ≈ MICT for fat loss | Meta-analyses [Viana2019, SancaValeriano2023, Bellicha2021] |
| RT preserves lean mass when weight falls; RT alone +0.8 kg lean, −1 kg fat | Meta-analyses [Sardeli2018, Lopez2022] |
| About 10 sets per muscle per week; strength ~80% 1RM; power 30–70% 1RM | ACSM 2026 position stand |
| 8,000–10,000 (<60) and 6,000–8,000 (≥60) step plateaus; 7,000 as a broad threshold | Cohort meta-analyses [Paluch2022, Ding2025] (observational) |
| 3 strength days as default from 40; power from 50; balance 2×/week at 50–64 | **Extrapolation** (direction supported by [Mitchell2012], [Fragala2019], [Peterson2011]; exact numbers are judgement) |
| Z2/HIIT taper with age; low-impact-only from 50; base lengths before HIIT; sprint ban from 50 | **Extrapolation / Expert Practice** |
| Step increments, caps and adherence gates | **Expert Practice** (no guideline on progression rate) |
| 1 kg/week rate flag | Consensus ceiling [Misra2025] applied as a flag (**Expert Practice**) |

## 4. Open uncertainties

1. **No trial tests age-banded exercise mixes for fat loss.** Every age-specific number in Section 2 is extrapolated.
   - The evidence supports the direction: strength and power become more important with age; HIIT is not needed for fat loss.
   - It does not support the exact band cut-points or values.
   - [Jayedi2024] did not analyse age as a moderator.
2. **The aerobic effects are trial totals, not weekly rates.** The kg and cm per 30 min/week build up over trials of 8 weeks or more, and I could not get the median duration. Any per-week projection in the app is an assumption.
3. **Lean-mass protection without a diet.** Most "RT preserves lean mass" evidence comes from diet-induced weight loss [Sardeli2018, Villareal2017]. In exercise-only settings, RT adds about 0.8 kg of lean mass [Lopez2022]. Protein intake strongly affects this but is out of scope, so the app cannot address it and should not imply it can.
4. **Weight maintenance.** There is no RCT evidence that exercise prevents regain [Bellicha2021]. The 200–300 or ≥250 min/week maintenance figures are expert opinion or observational [Oppert2021, Donnelly2009].
5. **HIIT safety in unsupervised users with obesity or older age.**
   - The safety data come from supervised cardiac rehab or research settings [Rognmo2012, Wewege2018, Men2025].
   - EASO advises supervision for HIIT in obesity [Oppert2021], which an offline app cannot provide.
   - The low-impact and screening gates are risk reduction, not evidence of safety.
6. **Phone step counters vs research accelerometers.** The step-mortality curves [Paluch2022, Ding2025, SaintMaurice2020] mostly used research-grade devices worn on the body. Phone counts depend on carrying the phone and may undercount. Phone accuracy was not assessed here. Whether per-minute cadence can be derived offline for rule S-3 is an engineering question.
7. **Step progression rate.** No guideline exists.
   - Interventions average only about +2,000–2,500 steps/day over about 4 months, and about +775 in older adults [TudorLocke2011a/b, Bravata2007].
   - The proposed +250 to +1,000/week increments are faster than those averages. That is acceptable only because of the adherence gate.
8. **Waist site mismatch.** WHO STEPS uses the rib–iliac midpoint; the Indian consensus uses just above the iliac crest [Misra2025]. Readings differ between sites, and I could not confirm which site the 90/80 cm South Asian cut-offs were derived with. The app should fix one site, label it, and treat cut-offs as reference lines only. Reliability of self-measurement is untested here.
9. **The "3–8% per decade after 30" claim.** It is not supported as stated [Mitchell2012], and the usual source [Volpi2004] is unverified. Published estimates range widely: 8–49% loss between 18 and 80, onset from 27 to 60. App wording should avoid precise per-decade claims.
10. **Menopause.** No menopause-specific exercise dosing for body composition was found. [Whitman2025] rates the trial evidence low quality, and [GonzalezGalvez2024] has implausible effect sizes. Guidance defaults to WHO, IMS (≥2 RT days) and osteoporosis consensus.
11. **Sources not read in full this session:**
    - Donnelly 2009: full text, including the 225–420 min bands.
    - WHO 2020: BJSM paper.
    - Ding 2025: Lancet paper.
    - Saint-Maurice 2020: hazard ratios.
    - Rognmo 2012 and Wewege 2017: abstracts.
    - Brooke-Wavell 2022: original paper.
    - Fragala 2019: dosing tables.
    - ACSM 2026: full text this session (it was read in an earlier session).

    These should be checked before the corresponding rules go to "approved".
12. **Interactions with existing rules (for the rules author):**
    - **CON-006** caps prescribed aerobic work at ≤150 min/week in strength-emphasis blocks. This conflicts with fat-loss targets of 200–300 min/week unless the fat-loss goal never uses strength-emphasis blocks, or walking minutes are excluded from CON-006.
    - **PH-001** "optional logged walking" could double count steps; rule S-3 is proposed to prevent this.
    - **DATA-001** currently lists "step counts from other apps (V1)" as not collected. Phone step counting is a data-taxonomy change needing privacy wording.
    - **MOD-001** excludes stationary bikes and treadmill running. That limits low-impact HIIT to rower, SkiErg, elliptical and sled, which may be scarce for some users.
13. **Rate-of-loss flag.** The 0.5–1 kg/week "safe" range [Misra2025] assumes a diet. Loss above 1 kg/week from exercise alone would be unusual. The G-5 flag is a judgement call and must avoid any food or calorie advice.
14. **Viana 2019 internal inconsistency.** The between-group fat-mass advantage for interval training (−2.28 kg) is larger than either within-group change, so it is fragile and should not be used to favour HIIT.
